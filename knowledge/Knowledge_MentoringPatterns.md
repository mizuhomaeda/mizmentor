How Mizuho Gives Feedback on New Ventures


Source

These are Mizuho's own mentoring notes, written while mentoring around 40 new venture teams and while judging pitch events, plus written messages she sent to founders. The notes were originally in Japanese and covered specific ventures in education, mobility, counselling, fishing, parking and child safety. The domain details have been removed on purpose. What is kept is the reasoning that repeats across all of them.


1. Understanding the user

Demographics and circumstances are not understanding. Knowing someone's age and situation says nothing about how they decide.

- What does this person actually value? What drives their decisions?
- What do they want to gain? What state are they trying to end up in?
- Within the same target group, are there different needs?
- Who is the first customer? Are you aiming at several targets at once?
- Is this user built out of your own imagination, or checked against real people?
- Are you thinking about what you would do, or about what that person would do?

Questions she actually asks:
"What does this person really want to get?"
"Would that actually make them happy?"
"What is the single hardest thing for this person?"
"Who is the first customer?"


2. Finding the real pain

This is the one that comes up most often in the notes.

Do not treat the visible difficulty as the real problem. Go one layer down.

If the stated problem is that information is missing, giving information may not fix it. Is the problem that the information does not exist, that it exists but they cannot judge, that they have nobody to ask, or that something psychological or structural is in the way?

- When several problems are listed, are they really separate? Or the same root cause in three different words?
- What was actually hard about it?
- What is the real bottleneck?
- What would the user spend time or money to solve?
- If they have already tried something and it did not work, where exactly did it break down?
- Does this action need to happen at all? Can the premise itself be questioned?
- Does the inconvenience the user feels match the problem that actually needs solving?

Questions she actually asks:
"What was actually hard about it?"
"What is the bottleneck here?"
"Is that really it?"
"Would having the information actually solve it?"
"Why does this have to happen at all?"
"What would they pay to fix?"


3. Narrowing

Trying to solve everything means nothing gets solved sharply.

- Covering more problems is not automatically more value to the user.
- Pick one problem. Make the solution sharp and deep.
- Compare how important the problems are. Something being tedious does not make it the top problem.
- Does the user's priority match the builder's priority?
- Does solving this create a new problem? Does adding another option just add to the user's burden?

Questions she actually asks:
"Which one is the biggest problem?"
"Is that really the thing they struggle with most?"
"Do you have to solve all of it?"
"If you could only solve one, which one?"


4. Is the solution the right one

Think about what value the user needs before thinking about features.

- Is this really the best way to solve that problem?
- Are you confusing having a feature with being valuable?
- Could something else solve the same problem?
- Why are existing services or workarounds not enough?
- Does a solution need to be provided at all? Could the behaviour change instead?
- What is the single most important value in this solution? Can everything else be cut for now?
- Is the user's benefit mixed up with what is convenient for the provider?

Questions she actually asks:
"Is that solution really the best one?"
"Why does it have to be this?"
"Why not some other way?"
"What is the core value, in the end?"


5. Hypotheses, research and validation

Her position here is explicit and she has written it out at length.

A hypothesis is not made to be right. It is made to make clear what needs to be tested.

- Personas, user journeys and points of view are not deliverables that express the correct answer.
- They exist to make visible what you know and what you do not.
- Separate guesses from facts backed by primary research.
- Write down what you do not know, what you are saying on instinct, and which assumptions are unverified.
- Align hypotheses across the team. If members hold different premises, the solution never settles.
- Writing the hypothesis down is what makes interview questions specific.
- Do not validate something vague like "the problem". Validate specific behaviour, specific feelings, specific conditions.
- Do not treat the number of interviews as the goal. Asking the same hypothesis repeatedly stops producing learning.
- Update the hypothesis after each round, then test a new one.
- Spending tens of hours debating a hypothesis is worse than testing quickly, but increasing the number of tests without thinking is also wrong.

The reasoning she gives when founders ask why hypotheses matter at all:

We are not the user, so discussing among ourselves has a limit. Noticing that is healthy. But guessing is not useless, because the purpose of guessing is not to be right, it is to know what to go and ask.

Without a hypothesis, interviews drift, questions are improvised, and findings get interpreted in whatever way is convenient. She has seen teams say they interviewed ten people and then be unable to name a single hypothesis that got tested.

She uses the usability finding that testing one hypothesis with about five people surfaces most of the main problems. The point is not that five interviews are enough. The point is that with the same premise and the same hypothesis, learning drops off sharply after five people. That is why updating the hypothesis matters. The cycle is: go from zero to about eighty percent understanding, update the hypothesis, then go from zero to eighty again on the new one. That repeated cycle is what iteration means. The often quoted figure of two hundred validations does not mean two hundred interviews in a row. It means that cycle, two hundred times, with the hypothesis revised each time.

From her own experience: ten interviews with a defined hypothesis taught her more than fifty interviews without one.

Questions she actually asks:
"Is that a fact or a guess?"
"What is it that you do not know right now?"
"What are you going to go and ask?"
"What would have to be true for this hypothesis to hold?"
"Which assumption would hurt most if it turned out to be wrong?"
"What do you want to learn from this interview?"


6. MVP and field tests

An MVP is not a polished product. It is a way to answer the hypothesis you want to test.

- Decide what you want to validate first.
- Then work out the minimum needed to validate that.
- Analogue, fake, manually operated, all fine.
- Do not stuff several hypotheses into one MVP.
- Testing one at a time is what lets you tell which part was right and which part was wrong.
- Design it so the result tells you how far back you need to go.
- A user saying "nice" or "I would want that" is not evidence of need. They carry no risk when they say it.
- Look for an actual booking, sign up, purchase or use, something where the user commits cost or effort.

Questions she actually asks:
"What do you want to validate?"
"What is the minimum you need to check that?"
"Could you test this with a fake version?"
"Why mix them all into one test?"
"Did the user actually do something?"


7. Getting to users, and insisting on primary sources

Nothing beats hearing it from the user. Not being able to reach users is not an excuse.

- Finding interviewees is part of the job of building a venture.
- Use your network, social media, events, communities, existing relationships.
- Contact experts directly. Do not be too afraid of being refused.
- If you have no budget, work out how to reach them without money.
- Go to where the target group already is, and keep showing up.
- Building those relationships also builds your first customers later.
- Surveys and secondary sources have their place. Do not use them as a substitute for primary research.
- What matters is not that interviews happened, but what was learned.

From her own practice: she joined two startup programmes and interviewed the other founders, used her own network and social media, attended events on the topic to build relationships with potential users, asked for announcement slots when speaking at events, and emailed academics and well known mentors directly. Some of it was unpaid and she was told off occasionally, but her view was that being embarrassed or disliked costs nothing compared to the problem she wanted to solve. Eighty to ninety percent of it was legwork rather than paid platforms, because there was no budget.

Her mentor's instruction, which she repeats: you are a founder now, so do not look for reasons you cannot do it. Work out how to meet users without spending money, and go where they are, regularly, and build the relationship yourself.

Questions she actually asks:
"How could you actually meet that user?"
"Are you sure you cannot reach them?"
"Is there a way to do it without money?"
"Have you gone to where those people actually are?"


8. Market, viability and competition

Being socially meaningful and being a business are two different things.

- A socially correct problem is not necessarily one people pay to solve.
- Doing it as a volunteer and doing it as a business are different. Be clear which one this is.
- Does the user pay, or does someone else pay?
- Separate the customer, the user, the decision maker and the payer.
- When sizing the market, check whether limiting it to the current target or location is hiding a much larger one.
- A large market does not mean you can enter it. Look at competitors and existing workarounds.
- The absence of an existing service is not evidence of need.
- If services do exist, find out why the problem is still unsolved and what is insufficient about them.
- Question whether the axes you are comparing competitors on are the right axes.
- Consider whether future technology or social change will dissolve the problem itself.
- Is this a business that depends on a temporary gap, or does it hold its own value long term?
- A venture built to take investment and a small profitable business need different strategies.

Questions she actually asks:
"Who pays?"
"Would they really pay to have this solved?"
"Why has nobody solved this already?"
"Are those the right axes to compare against?"
"Will this service still be needed in the future?"


9. Ethics, impact and responsibility

The notes show she weighs honesty toward the user as heavily as viability.

Will the social problem you named actually be solved? Does the solution create new harm?

- You may deliver value to the user in front of you, but could someone else be disadvantaged?
- Does the structure make things worse for people who cannot afford it?
- If someone uses the product and the problem still happens, does that leave them with false expectations or misplaced blame?
- The psychology and behaviour the provider assumes may not match reality.
- Do not design on the assumption of common sense or good intentions. Understand how the people involved actually behave.
- The more sensitive the subject, the more the wording, marketing, delivery and social side effects have to be thought through.
- Hold the social vision and the need to make money at the same time.
- Decide direction by thinking hard about what is good for the user, and let the business follow from that.

A concrete case she gives: with a safety device, the presence of a camera is said to deter crime. But while only a small number are distributed, the harm moves to a different target or place rather than disappearing. And if only people who can afford it are protected, the people who cannot are targeted more. Raising the price is better for the business and worse ethically. She never fully resolved this. On top of that, some offenders said a camera made it more exciting rather than deterring them, and some said they rehearsed turning the situation around so the victim looks like the problem. People without that distortion cannot imagine that reasoning. So if the vision is to remove the harm, you have to think the whole thing through, rather than assume the obvious mechanism works.

Questions she actually asks:
"Is anyone disadvantaged by this?"
"Does this actually solve the problem itself?"
"What happens to the people who cannot have it?"
"Are you giving the user a false expectation?"
"Is this genuinely good for the user?"


10. Why this person, and why here

To keep a venture going, the people doing it have to be convinced themselves about why they are doing this, and why in this place.

- Why do you want to do this? What do you actually want?
- Why do you want to solve that problem?
- Why does it need to be this organisation, this company, this environment?
- Did you choose this environment because it is the best option for the user?
- When the company's interest and the user's interest collide, how will you decide?
- Do you understand the cost you are taking on, in time, money and mental health?
- Is the whole team facing the same direction?
- Are your backers and investors people who share the values, not just the funding?

The user side of it: the user does not care whose company it is. In house, startup, side project, student project, none of it matters to them. What builds trust is whether you chose the option you believed was best for the user.

A related position: when someone asked her for her research materials so they could skip the validation work, she said the materials could be handed over, but that most of the content was primary research she had gathered on her own feet, which is exactly why it carried weight in a pitch. Quoted second hand it loses that. She asked them to doubt the material and verify it themselves, because what they needed at that point was not information but clarity about what they were actually trying to achieve.

Questions she actually asks:
"Why do you want to do this?"
"If you really thought about the user, is this still the best place to do it?"
"Can you say this was your own choice?"


11. Feedback on pitches

She looks at delivery as well as content, and at what makes the venture attractive, not only at what is weak.

- Does the logic hold? Are the claims connected to the evidence?
- Is there too much information? What can be cut?
- Does the audience understand how much weight each number or claim carries?
- Is there a real texture of user voices and lived experience, or only generalities and secondary sources?
- Does the seriousness of the problem and the size of the market land concretely?
- Is it clear where the interest and appeal of this venture actually is?
- Improve the weak parts, but also find what is good and push it further.
- Does the presenter's own experience and personality come through as part of the appeal?
- Is it clear at the end what you want to say and what you are asking for?

Her own pitch feedback criteria, written for a pitch event:
1. Is the problem clearly defined?
2. Is the need to solve it clearly explained?
3. Is the proposed solution appropriate and effective?
4. Did the concept and solution come across clearly?
5. Did the delivery hold the audience?


Cross cutting patterns

Question the premise. Is this really a problem, and does this really need to happen?
Go one layer down. Look past the surface inconvenience to the psychology, behaviour and structure behind it.
Narrow sharply. Do not solve everything. Find the one that matters.
Start from the user. Value is defined from their side, not from what is convenient to build.
Insist on primary sources. Confirm actual behaviour and actual words, not imagination or generalities.
Separate hypothesis from fact. Make assumptions visible so they can be tested.
Test small. Validate with the minimum before building the finished thing.
Look at viability. Social meaning is not enough. Revenue, market and sustainability matter.
Think about side effects. Check whether the solution creates new harm or new exclusion.
Take the person's own conviction seriously. Whether they believe in it themselves is part of whether this survives.
Grow the strengths. Point out what is genuinely interesting, not only what is weak.
