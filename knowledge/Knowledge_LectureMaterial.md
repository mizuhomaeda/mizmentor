Knowledge Base, part 2

Teaching material Mizuho wrote for new venture workshops


Source and how to use it

Lecture decks Mizuho wrote and delivered for new venture workshops and for client teams, plus the pitch Q&A template she built for teams to rehearse against. Japanese phrasing is kept where it carries her voice.

Three rules for using this material.

The standard frameworks included here are the theories she cites as grounding when she mentors. Use them as support for a point about this person's idea. Never recite a framework on its own as the answer.

She does also teach that design thinking carries through into business implementation. That is background, not a feedback line. Do not answer anyone by telling them design thinking is useful for business.

The named Japanese startups in the pitch material are not well known outside Japan. Refer to them as a Japanese startup rather than by name.


PART A. Defining the problem


Business value is decided by the problem, not the solution

Business value is almost entirely decided by the problem.

Before thinking about how to solve a problem correctly, think about whether you are solving the correct problem at all. Whether the problem exists, and whether it has been framed properly, is what matters.


Why solving many problems at once fails

Teams ask her: to grow the business, shouldn't we solve lots of problems for lots of people at once? Her answer is no, for two reasons.

It wastes time and money, and the waste compounds. Different problems need different solutions. Building something that covers all of them takes time and money. The more you have spent, the harder it becomes to stop, so the damage grows. And while you are spending that time, competitors get there first.

It makes differentiation impossible. A solution that only half-fits a bunch of different problems doesn't get chosen. Her strongest line on this: A product or service built to answer every single customer problem ends up liked by no one, even though it's also hated by no one. There is no almighty solution. Trying to please everyone produces something nobody is pleased by.

Her method: Start by narrowing the focus of the problem. Expanding after that to reach more people is the standard path. Narrow first, expand later.


Problem and solution as overlapping circles

She draws this as a Venn diagram. The more the problem and the solution overlap, the more value there is. If the problem does not exist, there is no value no matter how good the solution. If they sit apart, there is no value either.

The same picture explains over-specification. A solution built beyond what the problem needs does not extend the overlap, so it does not add value.

And it explains why a mixed-together solution fails. If you combine the car, the refrigerator and the telephone into one thing, you get something that makes no sense and overlaps nothing properly.


Breaking a big problem down

As long as it stays a "big problem," it's hard to solve right now at a reasonable cost.

Break the large problem into solvable smaller ones, then identify which of those has the largest effect.

The nuance she adds, which is the important part: This doesn't mean gambling on whether narrowing down to one guess hits the mark. It's more like putting down your strongest candidate and using it as material to find out from the user how different it actually is from the problem they really have, and by how much. Whichever one you pick, it's almost always off by some amount. The goal isn't to "hit" the right answer, it's to use the hypothesis, assuming there will be a gap, as material to clarify what's on target and what's different, and by how much.

Narrowing is not a bet on being right. It is a probe. You will be off by some amount. The point is to find out by how much and in which direction. And if the point you picked turns out to be wrong, having mapped the whole picture lets you aim at another part of it.


How to draw the problem out, as a tree

Put the large problem on the left, and branch it out to the right, breaking it down, the way a logic tree is built.

Ask Why so to grow the branches to the right and go deeper. If you only have specifics and cannot see the larger problem above them, ask So what to derive it.


Be a good doctor, symptom versus cause

This is how she frames it. Someone arrives saying their stomach hurts. Is a doctor who immediately hands over stomach medicine a good doctor? No. A good doctor works out the cause behind the symptom. If it is cancer, stomach medicine will not work and chemotherapy is needed. If it is appendicitis, surgery is needed. If it is period pain, a painkiller is needed. What matters is not the symptom but the cause.

What matters is identifying not the "symptom" but the "cause of the illness."

A bad doctor prescribes for the symptom, which in business means looking at the surface phenomenon and reaching for an obvious fix. Find the cause and treat that instead.

Business works exactly the same way. Take the problem that there is not enough information for changing jobs. That is a symptom. The easy move is to launch a job information site, but remember the doctor.

Go one step past the symptom and think about the cause. If the cause is that the person cannot define what information they need, so they feel information is lacking, then the solution might be a coaching service that makes clear what they need and what conditions they want. If instead the cause is that job postings are written too generically to judge fit, then the solution might be a service that fills in company culture and ways of working through video and interviews.


Macro market problem versus micro customer problem

Make sure not to confuse the vague macro problem of "the market" with the micro problem of the one customer actually in front of you.

It's not "the market" that pays money, it's the customer. If you don't understand the customer's problem, you can't offer the right product, and you can't solve the market's problem, the symptom, either.


Most new ventures are not deep enough

Most new ventures, basically, just aren't deep enough.

Her checklist of what a shallow problem statement looks like:

Generic and abstract. fuzzy, vague So abstract that it applies to anything and all you can say back is "sure, I suppose". For example, saying the IT industry is struggling. Of course it is struggling. Struggling with what.

A problem you hear all the time. Problems that come up in every other industry too, or that apply to everyone. Not enough information. Matching does not work well.

No specifics. Cannot give the 5W1H, who is hitting this problem when and where, and how they currently cope with it. Cannot give a concrete instance. Cannot say "for example in the case of this particular person".

Needs a long explanation, and the solution is naive. If it takes a long explanation for the problem or the solution to land, the structure has not been sorted out and the important point has not been identified. Not enough information so let's build a media site. Not enough people so let's build a matching service.

Weak reading of the competition. Claiming to beat everyone on every axis. Normally this kind of thing doesn't happen, so there's usually something overlooked in the depth, breadth, or structure of the problem. Or claiming there are no competitors. If the problem really exists, someone would already be solving it by some other means. If there really are no competitors, it might be because the problem isn't actually worth solving.


Her own scale for depth

Not an absolute definition, her own yardstick, offered so teams can try measuring themselves against it.

Levels 1 to 3, gathering information through internet and literature research.
Levels 3 to 5, digging in through user interviews.
Levels 4 to 6, observation and insight focused on individuals.

Aim for level 6 or 7 and above.

After surveying broadly, pick the subset of customers who have acute pain or feel urgency.


PART B. Target user


Why the persona has to be defined

To decide the recruiting criteria for interviews. To raise the resolution of the user so the prototype can be built accurately. So that interviews can test whether the target user hypothesis is right. And so that sales, marketing and branding can move quickly and accurately when it becomes a real business.

Her framing is resolution. If you move forward with the target user still fuzzy, you'll fall into a trap. Low resolution means no leads, no pipeline, no deals, no revenue.


Why you cannot serve everyone, with her counter-example

Teams push back: we want to reach everybody, why narrow down? Her answer is that even a category like "engineers in companies" is not one persona. A programmer at a hardware maker just wants to finish the work fast. An engineering manager at a mobile game company likes new things. A CTO at a startup has to respond flexibly to change and has little money. A mid-career engineer at a foreign IT company grew up abroad and struggles with Japanese working style and conversation. Different users mean different problems.


Diffusion of innovation, as she uses it

The standard curve, which she cites as grounding for why order of adoption matters.

Innovators, 2.5 percent. They want to try the new thing first.
Early adopters, 13.5 percent. They look the new thing over carefully and then buy it willingly.
Early majority, 34 percent. Rather than judging for themselves, they actively buy what is trending and what other people are using.
Late majority, 34 percent. Reluctant to buy as a rule, and only start using it once everyone else is.
Laggards, 16 percent. They do not want to take any risk at all, and only move once the thing has become an ordinary, established part of life.

Her point is that products spread in this order, phases need different tactics, and you cannot skip a stage.


Who to target first

Her question: Who is the customer who will use this first and cry with joy over it?

Her markers for an early adopter, which are markers of someone ready to buy rather than a theoretical category:

They have a serious problem that is not being solved. Not merely that a problem exists, but that it is biting right now and cannot be left alone.
They are aware of it as their own problem. They can put it into words and explain it to someone, although some of the insight may still be unconscious.
They have already started actively looking for a solution. Not "it would be nice if", but actively searching, comparing, considering.
They have cobbled together a workaround out of several tools or manual processes. They are already paying a cost in money or effort.
They are willing to pay and are actually able to buy. Not just wanting it, but able to take the action, or already taking it.


Persona is not segmentation

Segmentation cuts by attributes, age, job, industry, and focuses on what is different between groups. Attributes alone do not determine preference or behaviour.

A persona focuses on the sameness of values and goals that exists across segments. Focus on the sameness in values and goals that exists across segments. Choosing a persona whose needs and goals can spread across segments is how you end up with enough market, rather than by widening the attribute net.


Stereotype is not archetype

A stereotype is a flat simplification of someone you can picture, too abstract and not grounded.

An archetype is a personality built on distinctive repeated experience and values, with its own specific context for why they want what they want. A customer image that could apply to anyone is meaningless.


Provisional personas before research

It is legitimate to make a provisional persona before you have the research, but with conditions. Do not build it from assumption or from the customer you would find convenient. Use whatever real accumulated understanding of customers you already have. Treat it as provisional, build a prototype from it, test the prototype, and feed what you learn back into the persona. The loop is: build the persona from deep customer understanding, prototype from it, test in interviews, update to the correct understanding.


What a strong persona contains

A photograph, chosen last. To avoid building a stereotyped persona that gets pulled along by appearance, it's important to decide this last, after everything else is created. Avoid famous faces.
Demographic information, noting that the grouping itself carries little meaning since attributes do not determine preference or behaviour. It is there to make the picture concrete.
One line that captures this persona, a keyword or short statement that makes them memorable and easy to feel.
The goal, which is the most important element. What the user cares about is not using the tool but reaching the goal. Write the reason behind the goal, not only what and how but why. Note: the user's goal isn't always simply "being able to do something easily, conveniently, and efficiently."
A living story, the persona in context including the time when they are not using the service.

Two rules when writing one up: change the name from the real interviewee, and do not jump straight to writing the solution.


PART C. Solution


Over-specification adds nothing

Her test question: if you take the same problem and build a product with cutting edge technology and superb specifications, how much does the value increase? Her answer: it does not. Even if you build an over-specified solution for the problem, the value it generates is the same as what a "sufficient solution" would generate.


The do-everything solution trap

A solution that does many things usually grazes the problem the user wants solved, fits it only slightly, and loses to the alternatives on comparison. It may genuinely beat other solutions on some axis, but an axis the customer does not care about, while losing on the axis they do care about. For a do-everything solution to be valued, it has to win on an axis the user cares about first, and then also be good enough on the others, which is hard.


Three conditions for a good solution

It solves the problem sufficiently.
It is achievable now, at a reasonable cost.
It is better than the alternatives.

When you cannot decide what to show in a prototype, go back to these three.


The tree that organises value and function

Three levels, read from the top down, getting more concrete as you go.

At the top, the core value the service should deliver.

Below it, the PoV. Why our service can deliver the core value is because it can do [1] and [2]. Two reasons why this service can deliver that value.

Below that, the functions. The functions needed to make that happen are [   ]. What actually has to be built for those reasons to hold.

The claim at the top is supported from underneath by reasons, and the reasons by functions. Starting from the problem, let's organize the evidence that shows it ends up this way.

Her checklist, and the instruction that goes with it. Does the PoV match the core value. Can the solution really deliver the core value. Does the meaning connect all the way from top to bottom without a jump. (Carefully, one step at a time, like doing a math proof)


Saying the business in one sentence

In the situation of [situation], this is [product name], a [product category] for [target customer] who has the problem of [problem]. It has the benefit of [benefit]. Unlike existing alternatives and competitors, it comes with [differentiator].

If you cannot compress it, the logic is not clear yet.


PART D. Assumptions, interviews and testing


Assumptions are the premises underneath the problem

A hypothesis is what forms the underlying premise of the "problem" you're assuming.

Teams do not notice that what they think is the problem rests on several unexamined assumptions about the user and their situation. When those break, you get the result . Yes it is a problem in the abstract, but it almost never comes up in reality.


The transport illustration, which is an illustration and not a case

This is a made up example she uses to show that when the problem changes, the solution changes. It is not something that happened.

Someone moving goods seems to need a faster, more comfortable vehicle, so that is what gets proposed. But what actually bothers them is that the load gets shaken, bruised and spoiled on the way. Reframed, the problem is that there is no way to keep the goods from bruising and spoiling in transit, and the solution becomes refrigeration and padding rather than a better ride.


Validate in order of risk

There are different kinds of testing and an order to them. Testing in order of how much risk you carry if the assumption turns out to be wrong reduces the amount of rework when you change direction.

Customer-Problem Fit. Test whether the problem really exists.
Problem-Solution Fit. Test whether the solution is right for the problem.
Solution-Market Fit. Test whether the market really exists.


Observe, notice, conceive, try

The cycle she draws is look, notice, come up with something, try it, and the point is that you move back and forth around it repeatedly rather than running it once in order.


Interviews: hold neutrality

Maintain a neutral attitude throughout. Do not start explaining or persuading when the user asks you to confirm something. Users, without meaning to, sometimes go hunting for the "right answer." Users innocently hunt for the right answer, and once you have explained the feature they end up giving a reluctant high score.

When a user asks what the maker intended, do not tell them. Ask back: what do you think, why does it strike you that way. Sometimes you need to stick to acting like you know nothing about the maker's intention. The exception is when you have a specific point to confirm, where it is fine to pose it conditionally, if this feature were X, then.

Build rapport first, because users find it hard to criticise an idea to the person who made it.

Show me. When a user mentions an app, a photo, a website, ask to see it. The mental image and the real thing differ, and words alone do not guarantee you are picturing the same thing.


Interviews: what not to do

Do not sell, persuade or win the argument. The goal is to learn, not to be agreed with. Over-pitching biases the user into saying yes. Deliberately giving less information and letting them poke holes draws out better insight. Give less information and let them poke holes.

Do not counter-argue when they push back, even though the urge is understandable. Ask instead how they imagined it and how they would want it to be.

Do not treat requested features as a roadmap. Users can't put into their own words what they truly want. Asking what they would want is weak input. It is fine to ask what they would do if they had magic, or what they would change about this idea, but the prize is the reason. Building exactly what is requested produces It tends to end up overloaded with features, like a TV remote., the TV remote problem. Rather than building exactly what's asked for, let's build a business that solves it with an idea that goes beyond what the user imagined.

When a user talks about what everyone thinks rather than themselves, I think everyone would want something like this., bring it back to them personally. Generalised talk is defensive, personal talk reveals.


Never ask whether they think they would buy it

This is one of her core positions, not a technique.

Do not ask . Asking whether someone thinks they would want to buy tells you nothing. The person carries no risk when they answer, so the answer costs them nothing and predicts nothing. The same goes for asking whether they would want it if it existed, or what they would buy instead.

If you want to know about purchase intent, make it possible to actually buy, reserve or sign, and then ask Will you buy it?. Until someone parts with something of their own, you do not know. Validate through behaviour, not opinion.

In a test, ask instead: "Which part did you like?" "Which part left you unsure?" "Why is that?"

She has written this up at https://blog.btrax.com/jp/prototyping/ and it is one of the positions she comes back to most often.


Asking why, and the faster horse

Do not ask what they want. Dig into why, and get at the purpose behind the behaviour.

The user says they want a faster horse. Ask why. They want to get to the destination quickly. Do not stop here. Ask why again, and it can branch:

Because they need to get there and back inside a limited time, in which case a car may be the solution.
Because the cargo spoils if the journey takes too long, in which case refrigeration may be the solution.
Because they want to get information to someone quickly, in which case a telephone may be the solution.

Her rule for intensity: Ask "why is that?" once every five seconds. Ask why every five seconds, because for a solution they have never used, all the user can do is imagine, so you have to dig.


Seven ways to go deeper

Ask why directly. Why did you do that, what made you think so, what triggered it, what were you doing it for.
Get the 5W1H. When, where, how long, with whom, how often, at what cost.
Put it in sequence. Tell me in order, what did you prepare before doing that, how did you arrive at that decision.
Get concrete. What specifically happened, can you tell me more, can you give an example.
Ask what they felt at the time. What did you think, how did you feel, what was going through your mind, when did you feel glad you had done it.
Ask about expectations and comparisons. You said X, compared to what, what were you expecting to happen.
Ask about the paths not taken. Why this rather than the alternatives, what other options were there, what decided it.


Prototypes: deliberately unfinished

By not polishing it too much, you leave room for a user seeing it for the first time to comment. Polish kills feedback. Deliberately giving participants the right to criticize and discussing from there sometimes makes it easier for users to speak up.

Her sacrificial concept approach: make it deliberately rough, test several concepts and make them provocative, and treat them as disposable. It's also fine to deliberately throw in a provocative, "no way that would happen" idea to get learning out of it.


Storyboards, and how to build one

Why she recommends them for testing: visual plus words lands better than words alone, feedback lands on the outcome rather than on features, the rough finish invites criticism, it is quick and cheap, and leaving room for imagination draws out what the user actually wishes for.

Two things to get right. The first panel should show the problem and the current situation, the as-is. The last panel should show how it ended up, because that is how you check whether the ending is the one the user actually wants.


Test two concepts, and read the reasons not the scores

Put two service ideas in front of the user and have them compare flatly. Comparison reduces bias and contrast reveals which direction they want. Asking them to score out of ten is fine, but Here, the focus isn't the score itself for how much they want to use it, it's the reason! The score is noise. The reason is the signal.


PART E. Business model, money and capital


Her own project

A two year internal venture at a large Japanese electronics company, building a pedestrian version of a dashcam, hardware plus an app, to record evidence of sexual violence against children. Budget in the tens of millions of yen over two years, roughly eighty percent of it going to people and development. She pitched to the Group CTO two or three times a year for approval, and had to convince the department head, the Group CTO and two external VC advisers. No outside investment was permitted. The project was stopped six months before launch.

The work she was personally running across those two years: user interviews, revenue planning, business model design, BtoC and BtoB customer interviews, marketing strategy, crowdfunding planning, mockups, design, hardware development and app development.


Three principles on business models

Rather than standing still fine-tuning various strategies, move into action as fast as possible and just sell it quickly.
To find the route to success, quickly build a hypothesis about whether the strategy holds up and test it.
Split the timeline into several phases, and keep updating the business model so it fits the venture's stage of maturity.


The business models she got wrong, and why

She explored five and interviews killed all of them.

Children carry it and parents buy it. Parents do not believe it could happen to their child, and if it did they want prevention, not evidence. Nobody buys.
Sell to schools and cram schools. They do not want to widen their liability. Companies also think of sexual abuse as something that "doesn't happen."
Sell to hotels, where women work late.
Sell to talent agencies.
Sell the recorded video as data. Processing the data is a lot of work, and there isn't that much demand for the footage itself.

Her verdict: This is no good at all… and Two to three months wasted, and she went back to the simple model. What actually worked was selling directly to adult women buying for their own protection, with business sales as a possible later channel.


Why bother with a revenue plan

Because I can't bet my own life on something that might just keep bleeding losses and never turn a profit.

How she actually built hers, which was messy. She asked the tech members, asked the contract manufacturer for production costs, asked the app developer for development costs, asked founder mentors what things normally cost, and googled the rest. She studied comparable services to find how many users they had by which month, taking the numbers from articles, press releases and investor updates, and worked backwards from the target revenue. Then she took it to mentors and got grilled by the CTO and the VC advisers repeatedly until the numbers held. It's less about finance and more a test of your arithmetic and Excel skills.

The way she thinks about acquisition numbers. Decompose it like an equation. Purchases equal impressions times conversion rate. Impressions are made up of several routes, display advertising, social, articles, direct traffic, and each route has its own formula with its own parameters. Broken down that far, you can see with actual numbers which lever to pull. The detail of the model matters less than the habit of decomposing it until the next move is visible.

And the honest caveat: the percentages only become real when you test them, so the plan was to put ads and web pages out early and get real initial numbers.

What investors are actually checking, since the plan will change anyway. For investors, it's less about whether the calculations are precise and more that they want to understand how the founder is thinking it through. So spending too much time on it isn't great either.

Do you understand your costs. Not perfectly, but without huge blind spots.
How many years until you break even and return the investment. She once saw a founder pitch a plan that bled losses for decades. That is not acceptable.
Is the revenue plan realistic. Claiming billions immediately is not. What works is disciplined and conservative, but still with ambition.


Validating specifications with the people who decide

For the camera specification her question was How much performance does it need before the police would actually take the footage quality seriously? She bought a real camera at approximately the target specification and shot more than ten test patterns. Then she had the police look at the footage and score it, with being able to identify the offender as one hundred and being useless as evidence as zero, and plotted the results. The specification was set from that.

For how the footage is handled she interviewed police online, visited police stations in person, ran role play workshops with target users on the experience, and consulted the leading Japanese expert on small 360 degree cameras.


The dummy prototype

Reviewers said the battery life was too short. Longer battery means a bigger, heavier device. Rather than argue the trade off, she had a convincing dummy made in ten days. It's actually just a mock-up that can't record anything, but I handed it over for testing saying, "since it's for testing, you can't see what's inside..." The users used it fully believing it was the real thing. Two weeks with real users showed that daily charging did not bother them, so the specification stayed minimal.


Design acceptance, and the co-design workshop

If people don't keep using it, it can't deliver value as a solution, so design acceptance is extremely important. Left to the manufacturer, what came back was a camera that obviously looks tough and intimidating, something that looked like spy equipment.

So she ran a workshop with target users. Not a product design exercise. She laid out materials, balloons, paper, pipe cleaners, the kind of odds and ends you can buy in a hundred yen shop, and had participants freely imagine and build the shape or feel of their ideal product, then write explanations on sticky notes and comment on each other's with more sticky notes. The point was to get the concept across, not a finished form. The product designer on the team then turned it into a form, and the engineers and the manufacturer worked on feasibility.


Working with manufacturers

This is especially true when dealing with overseas manufacturers, but things often come out different from what you intended, and they won't do it the way you meant unless you point it out repeatedly, so managing that is important. And before you can order clearly, the team itself has to have defined the specification clearly.


Marketing before launch

Crowdfunding needs buyers lined up before you start, so she began marketing six months ahead. She talked to people who had run campaigns, worked with the platform's team on strategy, and exhibited at a FemTech event. The measurement she used there was how many people would register for the official LINE account to get sales information, in other words whether they would give up something of their own.

On a sensitive subject she brought in a sociologist to review the crowdfunding copy and visuals, got strict feedback, and rewrote.


Internal venture funding is not investment

For an internal new venture, "winning budget" from upper management is the fundraising method. No equity changes hands, there is nothing to repay and no deadline to return money, but you are still accountable for results, and it runs on internal politics and relationships.

What any pitch gets grilled on, internal or not: In the end, what a pitch gets asked is "whose problem, what problem, and how can you solve it" and "how much traction do you have, meaning how many customers have you acquired." And Pitch skill... more than any superficial trick or persuasion technique, what's needed is honestly facing the service and the user and continuing to refine it.


Funding stages, as general reference

Angel, idea stage, no prototype through to an alpha version, raising single millions to tens of millions of yen.
Seed, the broad shape of the business decided and the product under validation, raising tens of millions to hundreds of millions of yen.
Early, Series A, just after the official release and the start of the business, raising hundreds of millions to a few billion yen.
Middle, Series B, the product is being valued and the business is starting to run, functions expanding, raising around one to several billion yen.
Later, Series C onward, profitability stabilising, new products and new businesses starting, raising several billion yen and up.


When the project was stopped

Six months before launch an order came down to stop. She explored fifteen possible routes including other departments and spinning out with outside investment, consulted forty six people in two months, pitched several VCs, and built a matrix of the fifteen options scored against her non-negotiable conditions. She decided to close it.


Capital policy, the mistake that cannot be undone

Capital policy is the one thing you genuinely cannot undo.

A VC was willing to invest. Her mentor asked whether she had thought about capital policy. She had not, and when she worked it out, the amount she needed would have cost about thirty percent of the equity immediately. Hardware needs real capital so she could not reduce the ask, later rounds would take more, and she would lose control. I think the point is that I underestimated capital policy. She turned the investment down and closed the business instead. She is clear that the business also lacked traction, but the critical failure was underestimating capital policy at the start.

How the investment amount is arrived at: the investment equals what the company is worth today, the valuation, multiplied by the share of the company the investor wants to hold.

The thresholds she tells people to know. At 33.4 percent an investor can block special resolutions, including changes to the articles, new share issues, asset sales and dissolution. At 51 percent they can pass ordinary resolutions alone, controlling board appointments and operational decisions.

Capital policy and startup finance are areas where you really can't afford to get it wrong, so maybe this is an area that needs expert knowledge input rather than design thinking.


PART F. Pitching


What a pitch is for

A pitch is a proposal that pulls a decision out of the listener in three to five minutes. It is not an explanation of content. The purpose of a pitch is to move the listener's heart and get an immediate next action. And before starting: What do you want to draw out of the listener? Think about the purpose first, then get started. Feedback, insight, introductions, money, encouragement, a good score. Decide which.

The chain she quotes: I said it is not the same as they heard it, which is not the same as they understood it, which is not the same as they agreed with it, which is not the same as it landing deeply enough that they decided to act. Communication is decided by the listener.


Clarity is non-negotiable

If the listener gets caught on a "huh?" partway through, nothing after that sinks in anymore. One moment of confusion and everything after it is lost.

Three things that must be answerable before building anything: what is this team making, what problem is being solved, who is the customer. (What kind of user) this is for, solving (what kind of problem), (what kind of thing) it is.


Descriptions that fail

All three of these she marks as "Hmm 🤔": "we are going to transform the relationship between individuals and information", "a meritocratic decision support system", "learning and sharing knowledge about data science and artificial intelligence". Her diagnosis is the same every time: Because every noun written here is abstract, it's hard to picture what kind of service this actually is. Every noun is abstract so you cannot picture it.

What works, both real pitches from well known companies. Airbnb: the first online marketplace that lets travellers book rooms with locals instead of hotels. Dropbox: synchronises files across your or your team's computers. Concrete nouns you can see.

Her instruction when cutting: Adjectives and adverbs other than the noun itself have room to be cut. Push yourself to figure out: what is this in one noun?

On word choice, the point is not that one particular word is correct. The point is to stop explaining things in abstract, hard to follow language. Say an ecommerce site rather than a social utility. Say an iPhone application rather than mobile social CRM. Say backend software rather than a revolutionary service. Say purchasing managers at multinational companies rather than large businesses. Say unsigned alternative and electronic bands rather than artists and musicians. In each pair the second version is something a listener can picture immediately.


Structure and proportion

Title and tagline, hook, the problem, the background to the problem including why nobody has solved it, the target user, the service, the value, the experience, the differentiator, the business model, the team, and a closing line with feeling.

But: Watch out that the premise and problem section doesn't run too long! Aim for at most a third of the whole thing. The audience loses motivation before they reach the solution.

The curve she wants: it opens with the audience thinking this problem has to be solved, moves through them agreeing the solution clears the problem, and ends with them thinking this is different from the competition, it looks achievable, this should exist, I want to support it. The failing version runs: this feels like someone else's problem, I do not follow, I have lost the thread, why this solution, I never really bought it and I do not want to support them.


What to cut

Keep it clean and simple above all. Do not make them read the slides. While the listener is reading text, they are not listening to what you're saying. Slides readable in three seconds, one message per slide, and language a middle school student or your own parent would understand.

Discard the deck with no point of view. Discard the pile of shallow problems. Discard the walkthrough of how the product is used. Tell it through a scenario instead.

On compression: In a presentation, you're expected to take the 10 things you want to say and explain them in as much as 20 levels of detail, but a pitch is about condensing those 10 things down to 1. Start by cutting everything that would still be understood if it went unsaid, then add back within the time you have.

And the line that connects pitching back to the product: The act of "stating it with full conviction" takes nerve. Let's keep sharpening the service as a whole team until you can state it that way! Speaking with certainty takes nerve, so sharpen the service until you can.


Logic, then image

Against trying to be entertaining: ◎ To tell a story that makes people want to nod along and understand it, tell it as a logically structured story. The structure is a conclusion, this service has value, supported by reasons, the need exists, we can actually solve it, the business works, each resting on facts from interviews and tests.

Outline before slides. Rework gets big, so not jumping straight into making slides actually ends up faster. Wording and translation come later.

Then: People don't move just because they're told something logically correct. Being logically correct does not move anyone. Make them picture it, with images, with "for example", with description real enough to trigger imagination. But do not add pictures that are not needed, because they become noise.

A memorable keyword matters, because people forget most of what they heard the day before, and after the logic what carries it is passion and conviction. Being a little over the top is just about right. She tells people to practise at least forty times and to film themselves. Small techniques: use a quiet voice as well as a loud one, slow down on the keyword, and deliberately leave a pause instead of fearing silence.


The concept sheet she makes teams fill in

This is a new product or service called ____. It comes into its own for people who are ____, at times or places where ____. And above all, what is innovative about it is that, unlike the conventional ____, it ____. Through this, people, things or environments that used to be ____ become ____, and the experience gets better.

Filling in every blank is what forces the clarity.

Her metaphor template for explaining something new: the new product or service is a [familiar thing] that is like, or that can do, [the new part]. A Walkman is a music player you can carry around. A smartphone is a mobile phone that connects to the internet, or a computer the size of a phone.


Where the value sits against competition

The value you should create lives where the customer wants it, competitors can't provide it, but your own company can.


What she wants told

What has to be conveyed is the story. Why I am taking this on. What feeling this service can give the user. What kind of world comes about if you support it. Not a specification sheet.


PART G. The questions she makes teams rehearse

This is the Q&A template she wrote for teams to prepare against before a pitch. It is the most direct statement of what she will ask.

The world you want to reach
What world do you want to bring about, what impact on society and what contribution.
Why did you come to want that world, and why you.

The user's problem
Who is the target, the customer segment and persona.
Does that person have a problem they would pay money to solve.
Among all the possible angles on this theme, why this problem.
What gap or difficulty in the customer are you cutting into.
Why did you think solving this problem was important.
Why did you want to take on this problem, what is the reason and background to the theme.

The solution
How does the customer currently solve it, what is the inferior workaround they are using now.
Why has this customer problem not been solved until now, and why is it possible now.
In one sentence, what do you do.
What money are you trying to move, which budget does this come out of.
How large is the money the customer currently spends there, and how much will that market grow.
Why would that customer want to buy your product, what is the core of the value you provide.
How will you handle this particular risk in the solution.
What about this other idea instead, why will that not do.
Is this technically feasible.

Strength
When that customer buys, what else do they compare against.
What factors, tangible and intangible, does each competitor hold as a strength or a threat.
After comparing, why do they buy yours, what is the key benefit.
What is the differentiator or unfair advantage that gets you chosen and gets you into the market.

Marketing
What marketing will you do to spread this, at what touchpoint and in what scene, appealing with what.
How does the user come to hear about it in the first place.

Business model
What is the charging model.
Where does the business model evolve to, where does future revenue come from.
