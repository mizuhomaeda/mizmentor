# Persona gap log

A record of where the agent's answers differ from how I would actually answer. Method: run a test conversation, write out what I would have said at the point where the agent felt wrong, then compare. The findings here are the input for the next update to the knowledge base.

## Case 1. Dog walking app, already built, low repeat use

What I would have said

I would first ask what value this app actually provides. Is it an emergency fix, or a special walk that gives the dog an experience it would not otherwise get? In what situation, and for what value, is money being paid? Then I would separate whether that value hypothesis is only imagined or actually being delivered. If it is imagined, go and check whether users are really coming for that value. If what users want is different, either pivot to their real need, or, if that conflicts with your own vision, keep the vision and redesign both the value and the way it is delivered: the app itself, the experience, the marketing.

Gaps found

1. Level of the question. The agent stayed inside the retention number. I go underneath it to what the money is being paid for. Without that, you cannot even judge whether low repeat use is a problem.
2. The agent accepted the founder's framing. The founder asked "should we add a subscription", and the agent argued about the subscription. I would replace the question.
3. The agent gave one route. My reasoning branches: imagined or verified, aligned with users or not, aligned with the founder's vision or not.
4. The agent stated causes it could not know as fact ("the habit-forming nature is missing").
5. The agent never treated the founder's own vision as a factor. It always treats the user side as the correct answer.
6. Every action the agent proposes is "go and ask N users about X". My first action is a thinking step: put the value and the situation into words. Research comes after.
7. When the founder pushed back, the agent moved sideways instead of returning to the value question, even though the pushback was itself a claim about value.

## Case 2. Food waste in student housing, no solution yet

What the agent got right

"Before we decide if this is worth building, we have to find out if anyone cares enough to pay for a solution." That is close to what I would say first.

What I would have said

I would ask who else is troubled enough by this to pay for it. Who gains, as directly as possible, when it is solved. For example, if the student housing pays for waste collection by volume, the way a restaurant pays for industrial waste, or if it pays for a collection service or for staff, then reducing the volume or the frequency benefits someone. Social problems are hard because often nobody will pay to solve them, or the person who would pay is hard to see. That is exactly why it is worth looking for them.

Gaps found

8. The agent assumed throughout that the person who pays is the student. It never separated the person with the problem from the person who would pay.
9. The agent used "does the user feel pain" as the test for whether a business exists. I treat that as one question, not the whole test, because the money can come from someone else.
10. When the founder said people do not care, the agent closed the idea down ("you are wasting your time"). I would open it out and look for another beneficiary.

## Case 3. Internal tool for insurance sales staff, pitching to a division head

What I would have said

The logic is broadly right, but saying that time saving reads as a cost rather than a gain is off. In a company, time saved normally converts into lower labour cost, or into staff being able to do other work, which is a productivity gain, and that is easy to imagine. The real question is what problem the person holding the budget feels about this organisation, and whether lower labour cost or higher productivity would solve it, and how you can bridge the two with logic. I would research the division head's needs rather than the end user's, and focus on solving those. When the end user and the person paying are different people, you have to solve the payer's problem first.

Gaps found

11. The agent stated a business premise that is wrong, or at least too strong: that a pitch promising only time saving will be read as an expense. Inside a company, time converts to labour cost and productivity. I would treat that as already understood and move past it.
12. The agent named the division head as the decider but never suggested finding out what that particular person needs. It described what managers in general care about instead. Generic knowledge was used in place of research on the specific person.
13. The research target stayed with the twelve sales staff who were already in the conversation. When the payer and the user are different, the payer comes first.
14. The agent asked the founder to produce the link to a business outcome before anyone had established what the division head's problem is. You cannot build the bridge before you know what is on the far side.

## Case 4. Construction delivery status tool, strong evidence already, asking whether to pilot

This one I mostly agreed with. The agent spotted that the suppliers have no reason to enter the data, and told them to go and talk to the supply side before piloting. I would say the same.

What I would have added

This is a platform business, where the service sits between two sides. Platforms have a chicken and egg problem, so both sides have to be in place, but the rule of thumb is to gather the supply side first and only then put it in front of the side that wants to receive it. If someone arrives to get something and there is nothing there, they leave. So understanding the needs and the pain of the supply side, and making sure they get value too, is extremely important.

Gaps found

15. The agent reached the right action, but only by reasoning from this one case. It did not name the pattern. I would say outright that this is a platform, that platforms have a chicken and egg problem, and that supply comes first, which is what makes the order of work obvious instead of a judgement call.

Note for when the rules are written: this does not contradict gap 12. In case 3 the agent used a general claim about managers in place of finding out what one specific person needs. Here the missing thing is a structural pattern that explains why the work goes in a particular order. Naming a pattern is wanted. Substituting a general claim for research is not.

## Case 5. Ceramic artist, wants to teach a six-month course, people only buy the two-hour trial

Mostly agreed. The last piece of advice is where another direction could have been offered.

What I would have said

There are mainly two routes. One is the agent's answer: meet the demand that exists, offer the one-off session, then draw a roadmap towards the long-term student you actually want to grow, and design over a long horizon how you move someone from the first to the second.

The other is that if you want to run the long intensive course, your target, or the field where you are marketing it, may simply be wrong. I would ask them to think about extreme users. What kind of person could plausibly want to learn something like this over a long period? For example, people on a six-month cruise. There is no signal out at sea and time is left over, so there may be people who would take part in exactly that setting. I want them to come up with groups outside the ones they already have in mind, with the constraints on their thinking removed.

Overdo that and you end up chasing a bluebird, so it has a limit. The point is not to treat "get people to commit for six months" as fixed. It is a means. If the vision is, say, to increase the number of people who genuinely acquire the craft, and I am guessing at that, then the question is whether there is a better means than six months. This is the route I would push, and the one I would most want them to think about.

Gaps found

16. The agent gave one route. I hold at least two, and I say which one I would push and why.
17. The agent treated the six-month course as fixed and designed a funnel to fill it. It never separated the vision from the means. The vision is what they want to cause, and six months is only one way to cause it. This is gap 5 in a sharper form: in case 1 the agent ignored the founder's vision entirely, and here it protected the means while never asking what the vision underneath it actually is.
18. The agent never widened the target. It worked only with the people already walking into the studio. No extreme users, no removing the constraints on who this could be for.
19. The agent attached no limit to its own advice. When I suggest looking outside the existing audience, I say in the same breath that going too far becomes chasing a bluebird. The agent states its advice without a boundary.
